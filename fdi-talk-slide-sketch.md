## Slide 2: Framework: Effects of FDI on the Host Country (1:00)

Key message: FDI affects a host through three channels. Each effect depends on the type of FDI, the sector and the time horizon.

On slide:
- Product market: trade, output, competition
- Factor market: capital, labour, wages
- Spillovers: technology, skills, linkages
- Bottom line: effects depend on FDI type, sector and timeframe

Visual: three boxes with the bottom line beneath. Redraw the diagram. Do not copy the lecture graphic.

Speaker notes: Host effects arrive through three channels. The same investment can help through one channel and hurt through another. That is why the sector and the type of FDI matter.

Lecture: L1, "Effects of FDI: host and home"; "Transmission mechanism"; "Things to consider when looking at the FDI effects".

## Slide 3: Why Data Centers Are a Distinct Case (1:00)

Key message: Data centers are capital-heavy services with few permanent jobs. Evidence from manufacturing may not transfer.

On slide:
- Virginia (GMU brief citing JLARC, 2024): about 74,000 jobs a year, of which about 59,000 are construction and about 15,000 operations
- About 50 operating staff per facility
- Thailand: 521.2 billion baht from 28 data-center projects in H1 2025 (BOI release 92/2568)
- Course evidence: manufacturing has strong linkages; services give little spillover support

Visual: paired bars, construction jobs against operations jobs (Virginia). [chart pending]

Speaker notes: Investment is large, but permanent employment is small. Most spillover evidence in the course comes from manufacturing, so we should test whether it applies here.

Lecture: IF, Alfaro (2003) slide and the lecturer's notes on sector effects; L3, "Spillovers and linkages".

## Slide 4: Why Communities Oppose (1): Shared Resources (1:45)

Key message: Data centers compete with local users for power and water. Part of their cost falls on the host community.

On slide:
- EGAT: operators requested about 3.5 GW against a system peak of about 40 GW (September 2026)
- Bangkok (JustPow, 3 Sep 2026): at least 247.2 MW across operating, under-construction and planned sites; water use about 2.23 million cubic metres a year (estimate)
- Chonburi residents: water scarcity and competition with farmers (Mongabay, March 2026)

Visual: bar comparing 3.5 GW with the 40 GW peak. [chart pending]

Speaker notes: The course uses crowding out for domestic investment when FDI raises the cost of funds. The infrastructure row of the spillover typology applies the same idea to local resources such as utilities and land. Power and water are the local resources at stake here.

Lecture: L2, "Crowding in / crowding out" slides; L3, Harris and Robinson typology (infrastructure row).

## Slide 5: Why Communities Oppose (2): Jobs, Information and Trust (1:30)

Key message: Permanent local jobs are fewer than the investment suggests, and the information gap fuels opposition.

On slide:
- US evidence: a typical county with a data center gains about 100 to 200 permanent jobs (Brookings brief, 2026; US, not Thai)
- Bangkok governor halted new data-center permits and ordered a review of the urban-planning law and EIA process (The Nation, 3 Sep 2026)
- EIA coverage for data centers is unresolved; residents report little information on resource use
- 4 September 2026: 166 projects paused while national rules are drafted

Visual: short timeline of the September 2026 pause. [optional]

Speaker notes: Residents see few permanent jobs and little information. The labour lectures show that foreign firms pay higher wages, and that employment effects depend on the skill mix. The permanent job count for data centers is small.

Lecture: L4, "Effects of FDI on host country wage and employment" (employment and skill composition); L2, "Government policy and practices".

## Slide 6: Thailand's View (1): Balance of Payments and Trade (1:30)

Key message: The government sees data centers as improving the current account and exports. The lectures show this depends on local content.

On slide:
- BOI: 521.2 billion baht from 28 projects (H1 2025)
- Imported equipment raises imports in the short run
- Foreign subsidiaries that import most inputs worsen the current account; local content reduces that drag
- An export platform can raise exports (the course's Toyota Thailand example)

Visual: two columns, one for positive effects and one for negative effects. Redraw. [optional]

Speaker notes: Investment inflows help the balance of payments in the short run. Imported equipment and inputs can offset that gain, so local content decides how much of the gain stays in Thailand.

Lecture: L1, "Balance of Payment Effect"; "FDI-Export link (+) in host countries"; "FDI-Import link in host countries"; "Short/medium-term effects".

## Slide 7: Thailand's View (2): Linkages and Spillovers (1:30)

Key message: Spillovers reach local firms through linkages and absorptive capacity. Thailand's auto sector shows how that works in practice.

On slide:
- About 720 Tier 1 auto suppliers; roughly half foreign-owned and about 30% foreign-Thai joint ventures (GTAI). Tier 2 and 3 suppliers are mostly Thai SMEs (Alvarez and Marsal, Mar 2026)
- Spillover channels: demonstration, labour turnover, backward and forward linkages
- Data-center rules: supply-chain and Thai staff plans (Por. 3/2569; exact terms to be confirmed)

Visual: pyramid of supplier tiers with the counts above. Redraw; cite the Thailand Automotive Institute. [optional]

Speaker notes: Spillovers depend on local suppliers. Many local firms in autos supply the foreign assemblers. Whether data centers create similar supplier chains is not yet shown by evidence. The government's supply-chain rules are the lever to watch.

Lecture: L3, "Spillovers and linkages" (backward, forward and horizontal) and "Structure of Automotive Producers"; L1, "FDI-Import link: the use of local content".

## Slide 8: Conditions for Net Gain (1:45)

Key message: Net gains depend on absorptive capacity, competition, cost assignment and additionality.

On slide (table):

| Condition | Course factor | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Absorptive capacity | Human capital; technology gap | Thai staff training rules (Por. 3/2569, to be confirmed) |
| Competition | Open markets to competing firms | Singapore: four operators at 50 MW each |
| Cost assignment | Does the benefit justify the cost? | Virginia: $0.011 per kWh tax from July 2026; 25 MW rate class |
| Additionality | Counterfactual | Georgia: 30% of data centers attributed to the exemption |
| Dependency | Concentration of FDI sources | Thailand 2024: China 21%, Singapore 11.3% (lecture chart; verify with BOT) |

Visual: the table above. No chart.

Speaker notes: The course lists conditions that raise or limit FDI gains. For data centers, these conditions can be measured: skills, competition, who pays for power and water, and whether an incentive changes what would have happened anyway.

Lecture: IF, the list of influencing factors; L2, "Given appropriate conditions" and "Does the benefit justify the cost?"; L5, "Counterfactual".

## Slide 9: Thailand Compared with Developed Hosts (1:45)

Key message: Ireland and Singapore set data-center rules with clear limits. Thailand is earlier in that process.

On slide (table):

| Dimension | Ireland | Singapore | Thailand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power | Generation matching import demand, sold to the wholesale market; 80% renewable share on a 6-year glide path | More than 50% green energy; liquid cooling; 100% energy-efficient IT (EDB) | Requests of about 3.5 GW; operators asked to fund grid costs (under discussion) |
| Siting | Dublin constrained; connections restricted in some areas | Jurong Island | Projects paused while national rules are drafted |
| Competition | Connection process due by 31 March 2026 (not verified) | Four operators at 50 MW each | Incentives: 8-year and 5-year CIT exemptions |

Visual: the table above. No chart.

Speaker notes: The course finds that gains appear mainly in higher-income developing countries, where absorptive capacity is greater. Ireland shows an export-oriented foreign sector with little home-market competition, and high tax receipts. Thailand can learn from the rules and from the trade-offs.

Lecture: IF, "Income level of host country"; IF, "FDI and the host economy: a case study of Ireland" (including "Little product-market competition"); IF, "Why was the country so successful in attracting FDI?"

## Slide 10: Synthesis: Effect by Criterion (1:00)

Key message: Data-center FDI likely helps the current account and tax revenue. Local jobs and spillovers depend on conditions.

On slide (table):

| Channel | Likely effect | Condition | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Positive in the short run; equipment imports offset it | Local content | Partly sourced (BOI figures) |
| Tax and incentives | Tax gains from profits; forgone revenue from exemptions | Cost test | Sourced (Georgia, Virginia) |
| Jobs and wages | Few permanent jobs; higher pay for skilled staff | Skills | Partly sourced (US evidence; no Thai data) |
| Spillovers | Weak unless linkages form | Absorptive capacity | Lecture evidence only |
| Local resources | Negative for power and water | Cost assignment | Sourced (EGAT; Bangkok estimate) |

Visual: the table above. No chart.

Speaker notes: The table condenses the talk. Each row maps to a lecture factor. The evidence column shows where the claims are sourced and where they are not.

Lecture: L4, summary table format (factor, effect and conditionality).

## Slide 11: Conclusion (1:15)

Key message: Data-center FDI helps Thailand when its costs are priced and its linkages are required.

On slide:
1. Test incentives against cost per job and forgone revenue.
2. Make operators pay for the power and water they use.
3. Tie incentives to local suppliers and training, and publish the results.

Visual: none.

Speaker notes: The answer is conditional. Data-center FDI is likely to help Thailand's current account and tax base. Whether it helps workers and local firms depends on conditions that policy can set now.

Lecture: "To sum-up" slides in L1, L3 and L5; IF closing slides.
