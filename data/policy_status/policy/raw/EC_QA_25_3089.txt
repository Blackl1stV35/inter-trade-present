                             European Commission - Questions and answers

Questions and answers on the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
(CBAM)

Strasbourg, 17 December 2025

Why is the scope of CBAM extended to downstream products?
CBAM currently applies to several basic material goods: aluminium, cement, electricity, fertilisers,
hydrogen, and iron and steel. It puts a price on the embedded emissions of imported products. This
ensures equal treatment to basic materials produced in the EU, where an equivalent carbon price is
paid under the EU's Emissions Trading System (ETS).

EU producers that use these basic materials as input to produce goods further down the value chain
(downstream goods), can be faced with increased costs because of the gradual phasing in of CBAM,
and the gradual phasing out of ETS free allowances.

The vast majority, 94%, of the downstream goods concerned are industrial supply chain products with
a high (on average 79%) steel and aluminium content, used in heavy machinery and specialised
equipment, such as base metal mountings, cylinders, industrial radiators, or machines for casting. A
small share, 6%, of the downstream goods concerned are also household's goods, such as washing
machines.

An EU producer of those selected downstream products could face higher costs of input materials and
components because of the introduction of carbon pricing on both imported goods (via CBAM) and EU-
sourced goods (via the EU ETS).

This can lead to carbon leakage, which occurs when production is moved to third countries with less
ambitious climate policies or when EU produced goods are replaced by carbon-intensive imports.

Acknowledging this risk, Article 30 of the CBAM Regulation requires the Commission to identify
downstream products at risk of carbon leakage. On this basis, and in line with the commitment in the
`European Steel and Metals Action Plan', the Commission is proposing an extension of the scope of
CBAM to selected steel and aluminium-intensive downstream products.

The inclusion of downstream goods in the scope of CBAM ensures that, for these products, the same
price is paid for emissions embedded in imported products as for emissions associated with products
produced in the EU.

Which products are covered by the downstream extension?
As announced in the `Steel and Metals Action Plan', the proposal focusses on steel and aluminium-
intensive downstream goods. It focuses on goods further down the value chain produced with steel
and aluminium goods that are currently in the CBAM scope.

In total, 180 downstream products are added to the scope of CBAM. These are goods with a high
carbon leakage risk and a high share of steel and/or aluminium content (on average, 79%
steel/aluminium content). The imports of selected downstream goods together represent, in volume
terms, about 15 % of the CBAM goods already in scope. In value terms this share is around 53%. By
2030, expected revenues from these goods are estimated to be about 20-25 % of the revenue
expected under the current CBAM scope.

Examples of affected sectors include machinery, hardware and fabrications, vehicle components,
domestic appliances and construction equipment. An example of a downstream good proposed for
inclusion is stranded wire, ropes and cables made of more than 95% stainless steel. However, the
proposed list also includes more complicated goods based on multiple CBAM inputs such as washing
machines, which consist of around 60% steel, 5% aluminium and 5% cement.
How were goods selected for the downstream extension?
Following the approach used for the original CBAM, the selection of goods was done based on an
assessment of the risk of carbon leakage of individual products, their climate relevance and the
technical feasibility of including the products in CBAM.

The carbon leakage risk was assessed using two indicators: trade intensity and cost push.

Goods that are more easily tradable may potentially be exposed to higher risk of carbon leakage. This
could happen through a displacement of production or replacement by imports from third countries.
The cost push indicator expresses the carbon cost (price paid for embedded emissions) relative to the
overall value of a product. This captures the degree to which the price of a product may increase
because of carbon pricing.

In addition, an emissions floor was used to exclude products with total emissions at sectoral level
below a certain threshold. This ensures that only goods with the highest climate relevance are
included in the CBAM's scope.

Finally, for technical feasibility, the material composition of products was considered. This was done to
ensure that CBAM basic materials make up most of the weight of products included in the downstream
extension, which makes assigning embedded emissions more feasible. In addition, supply chains for
these products are likely less complex compared to products where CBAM basic materials are only a
fraction of the total weight.

How will CBAM apply to downstream goods?
he methodological rules for attribution of emissions to newly added downstream goods ensure that
the boundaries for the calculation of emissions are always limited to those of the EU ETS had the
good been produced in the EU. In simple terms this means that only the emissions of the precursors
should be taken into account and not those of the downstream processes.

For example, car doors produced in a third country should face CBAM only for the emissions
embedded in the amount of steel plates that have been used for their production, and not for the
fabrication of those plates into parts nor for their assembly. Just like the producer of a car door in the
EU that faces a carbon cost only indirectly through the input material, that is the steel plates that
have paid a carbon price under the EU ETS.

While this delineation already simplifies the extent of monitoring that needs to be undertaken to
attribute emissions, and therefore apply CBAM, to these downstream goods, it should be recognised
that the extension includes a number of more complicated downstream goods notably those based on
numerous inputs and components, including from different CBAM sectors.

Resorting to actual emissions will be administrative difficult for these goods. Therefore, it is proposed
that the default values that applies to them does not include a mark-up.

Why are additional anti-circumvention provisions introduced in this proposal?
As announced by the `Steel and Metal Action Plan' of 19 March 2025, the CBAM anti-circumvention
framework is strengthened to address new risks identified by stakeholders and during the CBAM
transitional phase. These new provisions will make the CBAM more resilient to ensure its
environmental effectiveness. We want to facilitate trade, not to facilitate abuses.

The new anti-circumvention provisions go hand in hand with the closing of sources of carbon leakage.
The extension of CBAM to downstream goods and the inclusion of pre-consumer metals scrap (i.e.
materials discarded before reaching consumers) as a CBAM precursor will help make CBAM more
effective in addressing the risk of carbon leakage. Still, circumvention channels would remain and call
for additional actions.

The risk of misdeclaration of emission intensity will be tackled by introducing additional reporting
requirements in specific cases. This will better capture the composition of CBAM goods, as it impacts
the calculation of the CBAM financial liability. Targeted additional reporting requirements will also
address the risk that insufficient supply chain traceability can be exploited to under-declare emission
intensities.

The risk of abusive practices also deserves specific action. The Commission is empowered to
determine, in certain cases (combination of goods and origins), where sufficient evidence points
towards a high risk of abusive practices, that actual emissions can only be used where additional
supporting documents are provided to demonstrate that relevant producers have not engaged in
abusive practices. Once the Commission has established sufficient evidence of a high risk of abusive
practices, it will act within 3 months to address these risks. This will include imposing additional
evidence so that actual values can be used, without these evidence, country-specific default values
will have to be used.

How will the temporary decarbonisation fund support EU producers of CBAM goods in their
efforts to tackle remaining risks of carbon leakage?
This new fund will provide temporary decarbonisation support to EU producers of certain CBAM goods
to reduce their exposure to a remaining carbon leakage risk.

The risk is that goods from EU producers would be replaced by more emission-intensive goods
produced in locations without an equivalent carbon price. This would have the unintended
consequence of a rise in global emissions rather than a reduction.

The CBAM addresses carbon leakage risks on the EU internal market, but not the remaining carbon
leakage risk on third country markets. Operators of EU ETS installations producing CBAM goods
identified as being at increased remaining risk of carbon leakage will be eligible.

The fund will reimburse these producers for part of the EU-ETS carbon cost paid when producing
goods that are subject to a remaining risk of carbon leakage. EU producers will have to demonstrate
decarbonisation efforts to receive support.

The eligibility criteria build on previous carbon leakage assessments used in the context of free
allowances under the EU Emissions Trading System, which focused on trade intensity and emission
intensity.

How will the fund be organised and work in practice?
The fund will operate under direct management by the Commission, in close cooperation with Member
States.

The management of the fund will use existing reporting infrastructure and processes of the EU
Emissions Trading System as much as possible to minimise additional administrative burden.

This means that operators will send their applications for support to competent authorities of the
Member States, including, for instance, relevant information about emissions and the fulfilment of
decarbonisation conditions. The deadline for applications will be aligned with reporting cycles under
the EU-ETS.

To fulfil decarbonisation conditions, operators can either implement projects recommended in the
energy audit or invest in projects to reduce direct emissions in line with a climate neutrality plan.

Do you have more clarity on the Implementing Acts?
The Commission has adopted several rules (implementing acts), clarifying how CBAM applies from 1
January 2026. They take into account the simplifications of CBAM adopted earlier this year. These
clarifications will establish a predictable and transparent framework for all stakeholders, providing the
certainty that operators need while keeping administrative burdens proportionate and ensuring
coherence with the EU's wider climate and industrial objectives. The rules will be applied as from the
definitive phase and ensure uniform application of CBAM across the EU.

Notably, the Commission has defined or updated:

       Implementing act on the methodology for the calculation of emissions. This act provides
       rules for third country producers on how to monitor and calculate embedded emissions in goods
       they produce. This includes direct and, where applicable, indirect emissions. If the producers
       don't follow these rules, the importers of their goods will need to use default values.

       Acts on verification of emissions and accreditation of verifiers (one implementing and
       one delegated act). If embedded emissions of goods, as calculated by a third country producer,
       are not verified by an independent verifier, the importers have to use default values. These acts
       therefore set out the verification principles to be followed by accredited verifiers, and the
       conditions to grant accreditation. The rules are based on the EU Emissions Trading System
       (ETS) and internationally applicable ISO standards. Applicants for accreditation can be
       established in any third country in addition to the EU, but they need to be accredited by EU
       accreditation bodies.

       Implementing act on the price of CBAM certificates. This act outlines how the price of CBAM
       certificates is calculated and made available to declarants. The price will reflect the weekly
       average price of ETS allowances. Since CBAM certificates will be sold only from 1 February
       2027, for goods imported in 2026, the price of CBAM certificates will reflect the average price of
       ETS allowances during a given quarter.

       Implementing act on the rules for the definitive CBAM Registry . The new rules standardise
       and secure the registry, facilitate access for authorised declarants and persons with delegated
       access rights, as well as to customs authorities, and improve information exchange between the
       Commission and competent authorities. The CBAM registry will enhance communication,
       notification, registration, and checks, allowing seamless information exchanges among the
       Commission, competent authorities, customs authorities, CBAM declarants, applicants,
       operators, and verifiers.

       Implementing act on the conditions and procedures related to the status of authorised CBAM
       declarant. Following the CBAM simplification package, the authorisation procedure is simplified
       by making the consultation of other competent authorities voluntary. Additionally, the revised
       act reflects the new rule that importers who have applied for authorisation before 31 March
       2026 can provisionally continue importing CBAM goods until the date on which a decision on the
       application takes effect.

       Implementing act on default values: This act lays down the default values per Combined
       Nomenclature codes and per country of origin applicable in the definitive period.

       Implementing act on the scope of communicating with customs authorities. This act lays
       down which information is to be shared by the national customs authorities with the
       Commission, in which frequency and by which means.

       Implementing act on the calculation of the free allocation adjustment. While the free
       allowances under the ETS will be gradually phased-out, CBAM will be gradually phased-in
       between 2026 and 2034. This implementing act lays down how the number of CBAM certificates
       to be surrendered should be adjusted to reflect the extent to which allowances are allocated
       free of charge under the ETS. The adjustment for free allocation can be based on actual data or
       be determined on the basis of default CBAM benchmarks.

Further Implementing Acts are expected to be adopted in early 2026.

What is the impact of CBAM on fertilisers?

       CBAM will ensure that domestic and imported nitrogen fertilisers bear an equivalent carbon cost
       to prevent the risk of carbon leakage.

       The Commission has and will continue to monitor the impact of the mechanism on domestic
       fertiliser production and consumption. The objective is to reduce emissions in the production of
       fertilisers while maintaining food security and revenues of EU farmers.

       To take into account the specificities of the sector, a low mark-up of 1% will be applied to the
       respective default values in the first years of the definitive period. For other CBAM sectors, the
       mark-up will be 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027, and 30% in 2028.

       This measure will contribute to an array of actions aimed at mitigating the price increases on
       fertiliser imports and securing fertiliser supply in the Union. The Commission has acted to
       safeguard fertiliser availability by reducing use through 122 nutrient-management interventions
       (58 eco-schemes and 64 agro-environmental-climate commitments).
       It has diversified supplies and monitored markets via temporary MFN duty suspensions on
       urea/ammonia (2022/23), the Fertiliser Market Observatory, and trade dialogue with partners
       (e.g. Canada, USA). It is promoting greener fertilisers and decarbonisation through the
       Renewable Energy Directive (targeting 42% industrial hydrogen from RFNBOs by 2030),
       REPowerEU, the Clean Industrial Deal, and the forthcoming Industrial Decarbonisation
       Accelerator Act (including a voluntary carbon-intensity label).

       Moreover, the Commission is working towards the adoption of a Fertiliser Action Plan to ensure
       sustainable agricultural practices and improve the efficiency of fertiliser use in Europe.

       In addition, fertiliser goods, as well as agglomerated iron ores, will become eligible for indirect
       cost compensation (ICC) with the updated State aid Guidelines to be published by the end of
       2025. The Commission aims to ensure eligibility of ICC for fertilisers and agglomerated iron
       ores, while addressing overlaps between ICC and CBAM by an appropriate adjustment of the
       calculation of the maximum aid amount under the Guidelines for the concerned products.

       Moreover, the fertiliser sector will receive support under the Temporary Decarbonisation Fund.
       30% of fertiliser goods in CBAM scope will be eligible for this support.

What is the impact of CBAM on Ukrainian production and imports?

Despite the war and the damage to its economy, Ukraine is the largest exporter of CBAM goods to the
EU in volume terms (tonnes). Ukraine, which is primarily exporting agricultural goods, also exports
iron and steel (12,3% of its exports to the EU), as well as cement (0,6% of its exports to the EU) and
aluminium (0,3% of its exports to the EU). Exports of CBAM goods represent some 2% of Ukraine's
GDP.

The CBAM review report showed that the impact of the CBAM on the Ukrainian economy is expected to
be limited. The modelling shows that overall demand for Ukrainian iron and steel products is
expected to remain broadly stable, with export volumes increasing by around 1% compared to the
baseline by 2035. This small impact results from the relatively low emission intensity (compared to for
example China or India) of iron and steel produced in Ukraine. To recall, CBAM does not account for
the emissions associated with the electricity consumed to produce iron and steel ("indirect
emissions").

The expected impact on export volumes of cement, fertiliser and aluminium products is somewhat
larger in percentages (+ 24%, - 25% and - 3% respectively). These are however much smaller
sectors compared to the iron and steel sector, and in absolute values (euro) the impact is therefore
relatively small. These estimated changes should be seen against the background of broader volatility
in commodity markets that may result in more significant changes, regardless of CBAM.

Overall, as the impact on the larger iron and steel sector is relatively limited, and exports of other
CBAM goods make up a much smaller part of the Ukrainian economy, the impact of CBAM on GDP is
expected to be minor, at - 0.01% compared to the baseline by 2035.

It should be noted that the modelling could not fully take into account the impact of the war, nor
make assumptions on its long-term trajectory. Nevertheless, the modelling provides an indication of
the degree to which EU demand for Ukrainian CBAM products could be expected to increase or
decrease, given the specific emissions of Ukrainian CBAM goods relative to those of CBAM goods from
other third-country producers. Ukraine has a carbon tax on fuel emissions which could be the basis
for the introduction of a carbon price. The possibility for declarants, adopted in November, to use a
default carbon price will greatly simplify claims by Ukrainian operators under CBAM for a carbon price
effectively paid.

It should be noted that the regulation addressing the negative trade-related effects of global
overcapacity on the EU steel market does not exempt Ukraine.

How does the Commission's assessment of the impact of the CBAM on Ukraine compared to
other studies?
There are not many studies yet on the impact of CBAM. CBAM entered into application two years ago
with only reporting obligations until 1 January 2026. It is only then that the CBAM financial
adjustment will be introduced progressively. There is therefore still very limited data on the impact of
the CBAM available that would allow for accurate reflections on the instrument.

Studies have been mentioned which estimate that the CBAM would have a more substantial impact on
the Ukrainian economy.

However, these studies only consider the impact of a CBAM tariff on Ukraine in isolation. The studies
do not consider that the CBAM will also apply to other iron and steel producers, nor consider the
increase in carbon pricing in the EU itself from the phase out of free allowances.

The static approach used in these studies does not reflect the reality of CBAM, where the impact on
producers (whether in EU or third countries) depends on whether a producer is relatively more or
relatively less clean than other producers.

It is also worth recalling that the CBAM financial adjustment will be introduced gradually, in parallel to
the phase-out of free allocation of emission allowances under the EU ETS.

What is the economic impact of the legislative proposal?
Downstream products added to the scope of CBAM only make up a fraction of the total EU economy.
Overall, the macro-economic impact of the proposal is therefore very minor.

Output and employment in some sectors in the EU are estimated to increase. This is the direct result
of preventing carbon leakage: as production is not displaced to third countries, EU output and
employment are higher when compared to a scenario without a downstream extension.

Along the same lines, a reduction in carbon leakage decreases EU imports slightly relative to the
baseline. This is because the downstream extension prevents domestically produced goods to be
replaced by imports from third countries.

The impact on final consumer prices is very limited. Many goods added to the CBAM's scope are only
intermediate parts of final consumptions goods. Based on the impact assessment, the largest impact
at sectoral level is seen for construction goods. Impacts on aggregate consumer prices in most other
sectors are much more limited.

Measures related to circumvention and electricity imports are not expected to have any macro-
economic impact.

Is the CBAM compliant with WTO rules?
The CBAM is not a trade tool nor an instrument to protect the EU industry. It is a climate-oriented,
environmental policy tool which will be applied in a non-discriminatory manner that complies with
WTO rules.

Under WTO law, WTO Members can adopt measures to protect the environment, human health and
life as long as the measures have a genuinely environmental and not protectionist objective and are
applied in an even-handed manner.

The CBAM mirrors the EU ETS to which EU producers are subject. For downstream goods included
through today's proposal, emissions of production steps not included in the ETS scope will not be
subject to CBAM.

Under the CBAM, imported products will get no less favourable treatment than EU products. The equal
treatment is ensured by 3 CBAM design features:

       decarbonising efforts of companies exporting to the EU will lead to a lower CBAM payment;
       the price of the CBAM certificates will be the equivalent to the price paid by EU producers under
       the EU carbon pricing system;
       the effective carbon prices paid outside the EU will be deducted from the adjustment. No carbon
       price will be paid twice.

The proposal to extend the CBAM to some steel- and aluminium-intensive downstream goods
maintains fully the parallelism between EU and non-EU products sold on the internal market.
Analogous to the EU ETS, only the emissions of those input materials whose production is covered
under the EU ETS are to be taken into account for calculating the CBAM financial liability for importers
of downstream goods. This limits additional administrative burden for operators and importers and
ensures that the CBAM remains fully WTO compliant.

The temporary decarbonisation fund is WTO compatible, since EU producers of CBAM goods sold on
third country markets are, due to the EU ETS, subject to a carbon price which is not paid by third
country producers. Hence, they face a risk of remaining carbon leakage. The risk is that goods from
EU producers would be replaced by more emission-intensive goods produced in locations without an
equivalent carbon price. This would have the unintended consequence of a rise in global emissions
rather than a reduction.

The Temporary Decarbonisation Fund, by giving decarbonisation support to EU producers, merely
reduces the existing unequal treatment in terms of carbon price paid for goods which have been
identified of being at remaining risk of carbon leakage. It should be noted that the support is not
linked to exports, but to the production of certain types of goods which are at increased remaining
risk of carbon leakage. Decarbonisation support is awarded regardless of whether these goods are
sold on the EU market or on third country markets. Further, the criteria used to determine eligibility
of goods build on previous carbon leakage assessments used in the context of free allowances under
the EU Emissions Trading System, which focused on trade intensity and emission intensity.

What will be the changes for electricity as a result of this proposal?
The methodology to calculate the embedded emissions of electricity will be modified and streamlined.
Default values will be calculated on the basis of an emission factor reflecting the emission intensity of
all sources of electricity in the exporting countries and not only fossil fuels as is the case at present.
This will result in a decrease in default values that will in turn result in a lower CBAM liability. In
particular, the proposal will reduce the default values for electricity by over 30% on average, with a
level of reduction exceeding 60% for specific countries (e.g. Ukraine). The higher the level of
decarbonisation in the exporting country, the higher the reduction.

In addition, the conditions to declare actual emissions of electricity will be streamlined, making it
easier to rely on actual emissions. In particular, certain power purchase agreements involving
intermediaries will be accepted, it will no longer be necessary to prove the absence of physical
congestion in the network at the time of export, and the nomination of capacity will only have to be
demonstrated in the case of imports of electricity for which transmission capacity is allocated through
explicit capacity allocation. These changes, once adopted, will apply to electricity imported as of 1
January 2026.

Are any changes foreseen to the possibility of countries to be exempted from CBAM as far
as their exports of electricity are concerned?

No unilateral exemption is introduced. But a new provision is foreseen in order to clarify and provide
certainty to countries in the process of market coupling with the EU, namely the Energy Community
countries.

A Memorandum of Understanding may be signed between the Commission and the third country
concerned, once the Commission has assessed that this country has fully transposed the electricity
market acquis. This MoU will lay down details on the timeline for applying the CBAM exemption,
including in relation to technical work still to be carried out between TSOs, and for implementing a
carbon pricing instrument equivalent to the EU ETS as far as electricity generation is concerned.

Is this proposal another unilateral measure harming third countries?
We have to safeguard the competitiveness of our industry, delivering on our Clean Industrial Deal and
on our Steel and Metals Action Plan. We also committed on this at the European Council of October.
We are now delivering on it. Neither CBAM nor this proposal are unilateral trade measures. They are
environmental measures designed to avoid carbon leakage. We want decarbonisation in Europe, not
moving emissions elsewhere. The ETS and CBAM go hand in hand, we don't impose to third-country
producers what we don't impose to our own producers. CBAM is an engine for industrial
decarbonisation, and we will make sure it stays like this. It already works. Many third countries adapt
their policies: 80 countries other than the EU have a carbon pricing instrument in place (representing
2/3 of the world GDP), Brazil and Mexico have for example recently introduced carbon pricing. At the
same time, we are implementing simplifications and flexibilities (e.g. simplified calculation methods)
in the implementation of CBAM, and we are working on the concept of equivalence in carbon tax /
price deduction. We haven't created CBAM for the money it generates, this is simply wrong. Last but
not least, we have included in the proposal a new clause allowing for negotiated trade facilitation
measures (mutual recognition of trustworthy accreditation bodies) and new facilities on equivalence of
carbon price deduction.
