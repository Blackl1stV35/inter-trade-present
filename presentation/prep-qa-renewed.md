# Renewed Q&A: Cleaner Steel For German Manufacturing

The questions draw on the Germany report and the current slide deck. Group C treats "your country" as Thailand, a country the report does not cover.

## A. Report content

**QR1.** Under current law, 51.5% of the benchmark-based free allocation is still retained in 2030. What is the CBAM-liable share that year, and how does it relate to that retained share? (lecturer)

**Answer:** Under current law, the CBAM-liable share in 2030 is 48.5 percent. It equals 100 percent minus the 51.5 percent of benchmark-based free allocation that is still retained, so the two shares add up to 100 percent. The paragraph after Table 1.2 gives three milestones. The retained share is 97.5 percent in 2026 and zero in 2034, with matching liable shares of 2.5 percent and 100 percent. The report calls these selected milestones, not import-tax rates. The Commission's proposal of 17 July 2026 would slow withdrawal, but it is still under legislative consideration and is not current law.

**Sources:** Report section 1.6 (Table 1.2 and the paragraph after it), citing European Parliament (2026a) and Government Offices of Sweden (2026).

**QR2.** In Figure 2.1, each unit creates an external cost of 20. How does the socially optimal quantity compare with the market's 50 units? (lecturer)

**Answer:** The socially optimal quantity is 40 units, which is 10 units below the market's 50. The uncorrected market settles where demand equals private marginal cost, at 50 units and a price of 70. With an external cost of 20 per unit, social marginal cost becomes 40 plus Q, and the social optimum is 40 units, where the marginal social value is 80. The shaded triangle marks the model's excess social cost between 40 and 50 units. The report stresses that all values are invented, and it does not establish that cutting German output is desirable, because cleaner technology can cut emissions without the same output reduction.

**Sources:** Report section 2.1.1 and Figure 2.1, citing Lerskullawat (n.d.-b) for the externality framework.

**QR3.** In Figure 2.2, only half of the carbon-related import cost reaches the delivered price. What happens to imports, and does that depend on how domestic supply responds? (lecturer)

**Answer:** Imports fall in this illustration, and the size of the fall depends on how domestic supply responds. Of the gross carbon-related import cost of 20, only half reaches the delivered price, which rises from 40 to 50, and use falls from 80 to 70. If the domestic supply curve is unchanged, domestic output rises to 30 and imports fall from 60 to 40. If domestic costs also rise by 10, output stays at 20 and imports fall only to 50. The report adds that a larger domestic cost increase could lower domestic output. These are model inputs, not measured CBAM effects, and the half pass-through rate is imposed for illustration.

**Sources:** Report section 2.1.2 and Figure 2.2, which the report presents as the authors' simulation under stated assumptions.

**QR4.** Direct extra-EU iron and steel imports fell 56.2% from 2019 to 2025. What does the origin-based series show for the same period, and why do the two series differ? (lecturer)

**Answer:** The origin-based series shows a much smaller fall. In the Destatis compilation, non-EU-origin Chapter 72 imports were 5,050 thousand tonnes in 2019 and 3,919 thousand tonnes in 2025, a decline of about 22.4 percent, compared with 56.2 percent for the direct series. The two series differ because they answer different questions. The direct series omits non-EU-origin steel that is first cleared in another Member State and then supplied to Germany, so it does not describe every non-EU-origin supply route. The report therefore says the direct series should not be presented as all foreign-origin steel used in Germany, or as the tonnage on which CBAM certificates are owed.

**Sources:** Report section 2.4.1 and Figure 2.5. Origin-based series: Destatis (n.d.), GENESIS-Online table 51000-0007. Direct series: Eurostat (2026c), Comext DS-045409.

**QR5.** Rolled-steel market supply fell 28.7% from 2017 to 2025. What happened between 2024 and 2025, and how does that change the way we describe the trend? (lecturer)

**Answer:** Rolled-steel market supply rose 8.2 percent between 2024 and 2025, from 26.9 to 29.1 million tonnes. The report does not explain why it rose. The longer comparison still shows a fall of 28.7 percent from 2017, when supply was 40.8 million tonnes. So the trend should be described as a substantially smaller market in 2025 than in 2017, with a rise in the latest year, not as a fall in every recent year. Market supply is a quantity measure that can be affected by stocking, destocking and final users' activity, so it does not show a demand shift.

**Sources:** Report section 2.3 and Figure 2.4, citing Wirtschaftsvereinigung Stahl (2026), pp. 9 and 14.

**QR6.** Your report says the 2027 SALCOS plant milestone and the 2030 EWE hydrogen supply should be checked as one integrated chain. How much of SALCOS's hydrogen need does the EWE contract cover, and why does that matter? (lecturer)

**Answer:** The EWE contract covers around 6.5 percent of SALCOS's hydrogen requirements. Salzgitter and EWE announced it on 9 June 2026. The contract supplies about 10,000 tonnes a year, initially for seven years, with deliveries scheduled from 2030 through the hydrogen core network. That matters because the new route is scheduled to start in the first half of 2027, before EWE deliveries begin, and the report says much of the programme's hydrogen requirement still depends on other supply arrangements. The report does not name those arrangements or give their dates. That is why Suggestion 3 asks for the two milestones to be checked as one chain.

**Sources:** Report section 2.5.2, citing Salzgitter AG (2026, June 9) and Salzgitter AG (n.d.), and section 3.2, Suggestion 3.

## B. Slide content

**QS1.** Slide 3 shows free allocation shrinking. What share is retained under current law in 2026, 2030 and 2034? (classmate)

**Answer:** Under current law, the share of free allocation retained is 97.5 percent in 2026, 51.5 percent in 2030 and zero percent in 2034, so free allocation ends in 2034. The slide labels the first two figures as the factor retained. Slide 10 proposes a slower path, which would change current law and is still a proposal. The report's section 1.6 confirms these three figures and calls them selected milestones, not import-tax rates.

**Sources:** Slide 3; slide 10 for the proposed slower path; report section 1.6, paragraph after Table 1.2.

**QS2.** On slide 4, what happened to German crude-steel output and rolled-steel market supply between 2017 and 2025? (classmate)

**Answer:** Both measures fell. German crude-steel output went from 43.3 million tonnes in 2017 to 34.1 million tonnes in 2025. Rolled-steel market supply went from 40.8 million tonnes to 29.1 million tonnes over the same years. The chart runs from 2012 to 2025, but its labels show the 2017 to 2025 change. The report confirms these endpoints. It also notes that the two series measure different products and must not be subtracted to build a trade balance. It does not establish what caused the decline.

**Sources:** Slide 4, with slide 5 repeating the same chart; report section 2.3 and Figure 2.4.

**QS3.** Slide 6 lists four forces on steel supply. Which four are they, and which one covers the EU carbon rules? (classmate)

**Answer:** The four forces are energy costs, meaning power and gas prices; demand, meaning domestic and export orders; competition, meaning imports and foreign supply; and carbon policy. Carbon policy is the one that covers the EU carbon rules, which the slide names as ETS and CBAM rules. The slide says the data cannot separate these four forces. The report does not use this exact list, but its section 2.7 notes that energy costs, final demand, sanctions, trade-defence measures and inventories overlap with carbon policy.

**Sources:** Slide 6; report section 2.7.

**QS4.** Slide 8 says direct extra-EU imports fell 56.2% from 2019 to 2025. What were the import figures at each end of that period, and what were exports in 2025? (classmate)

**Answer:** Direct extra-EU imports of Chapter 72, which the report describes as iron and steel, fell from 3.342 million tonnes in 2019 to 1.464 million tonnes in 2025. That is the 56.2 percent fall on the slide, and the report confirms those figures. Exports in 2025 were 4.681 million tonnes. The slide's source is Eurostat Comext, and it notes that these figures are not the CBAM liability base, so they should not be read as the tonnage liable under CBAM.

**Sources:** Slide 8; report sections 2.4.1 and 2.6 (Table 2.1), citing Eurostat Comext DS-045409, retrieved 2 November 2026.

**QS5.** Slide 9 says announced steel plants still need capital, power and delivery dates. What examples does the slide give for that point? (lecturer)

**Answer:** The slide gives two examples. The first is ArcelorMittal, which announced on 19 June 2025 that it could not proceed with its plans in Bremen and Eisenhรผttenstadt. The report says the company cited market, policy and energy conditions, and that the case does not identify CBAM as the single cause. The second is thyssenkrupp's plan for about 5,000 job reductions by 2030. The report treats that as an employment adjustment, separate from about 6,000 positions linked to transfers or business sales, and warns against treating the total as job losses caused by CBAM.

**Sources:** Slide 9; report section 2.5.4 for ArcelorMittal and section 2.6 for thyssenkrupp.

**QS6.** Your conclusion slide asks for a slower phase-out. What does it propose for 2034 to 2037, and what should any extension be linked to? (lecturer)

**Answer:** The conclusion slide proposes a slower phase-out that keeps 15 percent of free allocation in 2034 to 2037, but only if investment is credible. It says any extension should be linked to the 2031 investment-plan milestone. The report does not mention a 2031 milestone, so that link comes from the slide alone. The report ties any extension to credible low-carbon investment, and it places the 15 percent within the Commission's July 2026 proposal, which would complete withdrawal in 2038. That proposal is still under legislative consideration, while current law ends free allocation in 2034, as slide 3 shows.

**Sources:** Slide 10, with slide 3 for current law; report sections 1.6 and 3.2.

## C. Thailand (your country)

**QT1.** Is it affecting exports? (classmate)

**Answer:** The report cannot answer this for Thailand, because it covers Germany only and does not measure Thai exports. CBAM applies to imports into the EU, so EU importers buy the certificates, not Thai exporters. Any cost reaching a Thai exporter would most likely come through prices or contracts, and the report's illustration assumes only part of the carbon cost reaches buyers without estimating how much. Certificate purchases begin in February 2027, though firms could adjust prices earlier. Thai sources differ on the current scale. A March 2026 IDE-JETRO policy brief says Thai exporters currently face little exposure in the first covered groups, while an August 2025 news article expects costs to hit exports directly.

**Sources:** Germany report, Sections 1.5, 2.1.2 and 2.4.5; IDE-JETRO Policy Brief No. 276, 24 March 2026, https://www.ide.go.jp/Japanese/Publish/Reports/AjikenPolicyBrief/276.html; Nation Thailand, 29 August 2025, https://www.nationthailand.com/business/economy/40054711

**QT2.** What effect do you think it has, and does it bring an advantage or a disadvantage for Thailand? (classmate)

**Answer:** My view is that the effect is likely mixed, and the report cannot size it for Thailand. The disadvantage is that exporters of covered goods, such as steel, aluminium, fertilisers or cement, may see certificate costs passed on through prices, plus closer supply-chain checks. The main advantage is that producers with verified lower emissions may reduce their liability, and a cleaner route can become more attractive when a higher-emission route faces a larger carbon cost. The report adds that a green label alone gives no exemption. A Thai news article from August 2025 expects a pricing edge for cleaner goods, but that is an expectation, not a measured result.

**Sources:** Germany report, Sections 1.5, 2.1.2, 2.1.3 and 2.4.5; Nation Thailand, 29 August 2025, https://www.nationthailand.com/business/economy/40054711

**QT3.** Is there any policy Thailand can use? (classmate)

**Answer:** Yes, options exist, but the sources do not confirm that any is in force. First, the draft act integrates a carbon price within petroleum excise taxes, and it lets carbon tax paid reduce domestic ETS auction costs. Second, IDE-JETRO recommends EU equivalence talks so Thai carbon payments can be deducted from CBAM certificate obligations, and EU recognition is unconfirmed. Third, Thailand's domestic CBAM, projected for 2031 implementation, targets carbon leakage from imports produced under less stringent rules. The March 2026 brief calls the act a draft, and May 2026 reporting suggested a delay to after summer. The report's Section 1.5 says CBAM complements domestic carbon pricing, such as the EU ETS.

**Sources:** Germany report, Section 1.5; IDE-JETRO Policy Brief No. 276, 24 March 2026, https://www.ide.go.jp/Japanese/Publish/Reports/AjikenPolicyBrief/276.html; QCIntel, 14 May 2026, https://www.qcintel.com/carbon/article/thai-climate-act-likely-delayed-to-after-summer-official-64860.html
